# Landing Page Strategy

*Notes I share after mentoring calls. All method, no case studies. Take it and test it against your own account.*

---

## The one idea everything hangs off

**A landing page doesn't have a fixed position in your funnel. Its job is whatever the ad in front of it hasn't already done.**

I got to this from running landing page tests. Stefan Georgi teaches the same law from the copy side in RMBC II, and he says it harder: the structure of a page is set by what the prospect already consumed. Not by the format's name, and not by the product.

So the ad and the page are one thing. Design them as a pair, or you'll keep testing halves and wondering why the numbers move for no reason.

---

## Where landing page ideas come from

Landing page ideas can come from two fronts.

1. Based on the angles, personas and partners that are working really well and are being sent to a generic LP
2. From research and seeing what works on other brands.

For #1, a really good way to add incremental spend to the ad account is duplicating the best ads and creating different LPs using different templates:

* Quiz
* Listicle
* Advertorials

This would need judgment on your end, because you probably cannot do a quiz to expand on a celebrity ad, although it can be possible if you're creative.

I've seen quizzes work really, really well for different types of ads. TOFU, MOFU, and BOFU.

What gets interesting is, since you can play around with personas, once you find an angle and a template that works, you can keep expanding using different personas, ages, even the picture that you're using.

For #2… if you are generating pages from scratch, make sure there's a network that ideally encompasses landing page creation, ad creation, and partnership creation. In that way, you are taking into account the whole funnel and aligning the whole organization to uncover a new untapped market for you.

## A listicle is a template, not a funnel stage

This is the most common mix-up I run into.

The **template** is the format: listicle, quiz, advertorial, product page. The **awareness stage** is who it's written for. Someone who doesn't know yet they have the problem, versus someone comparing you against a competitor.

Those are two different choices, and the same template works at either end:

| | Cold traffic | Warm traffic |
|---|---|---|
| **Listicle** | "10 signs your [problem] is worse than you think" | "10 reasons [figure] switched to this" |
| **Quiz** | Self-diagnostic. The problem is the subject, product shows up at the end | Same quiz, taken by someone who knows you and wants *their* version of the answer |

So "listicles are top of funnel" isn't really a thing. There are listicles written for cold traffic, and listicles written for warm traffic, and they're different pages.

What decides which one you built? Most visitors never scroll. So the awareness stage comes down to the headline and the first two or three items. Lead with the symptom, it's a cold page. Lead with the product, it's a warm page, whatever the rest of it says.

Meaning, you don't rewrite a page to change its stage. You rewrite the headline and the first three items.

## Pair the ad to the page on purpose

Work out what the ad already did, then give the page only the job that's left.

| What the ad already did | What the page still has to carry |
|---|---|
| Static image, product not explained | Everything. Problem, mechanism, proof, product reveal late |
| Short video that reveals the product and explains the mechanism | Very little setup. Open on the product, go graphics-heavy, close |
| Editorial or native placement, problem-led | Continue the article, then earn the transition to the product |

Two failure modes worth knowing by name.

**Warm ad into a cold page.** The ad already sold the product, then the page opens with "here's a problem you might have." The visitor is ahead of the page and leaves. Looks like a bad page. Usually the page is fine and the pairing is wrong.

**Modality mismatch**, which is the subtle one, and it's Georgi's example. An ad for a $99 product landing on the $99 product page. Same topic, same price, still broken: the visitor came to read something and you handed them a shop. Congruence isn't only about the subject. It's about what kind of thing the person thought they were clicking into.

## For cold traffic, the ad should sell the page, not the product

Most teams have this backwards.

Sell the quiz. Sell the article. Sell the diagnosis. If the ad introduces the product, then the ad has to do all the warming up, and the cold page behind it has nothing left to do. The visitor lands already warm and mismatched to it.

Quick diagnostic: if you attach a new cold page to existing ads and both efficiency and your share of new visitors drop, you didn't build a bad page. You pointed warm ads at a cold page. The fix is upstream, build cold ads to go with it.

## How much the page has to carry is mostly not a page decision

Georgi's funnel-factor work makes a useful point once you strip out the scoring. Most of what determines how heavy your page needs to be is inherited, not chosen per page.

* **Facts about your brand.** How aware the market is, how well known you are, how saturated the category is, how much social proof you've banked.
* **Facts about your product.** Price, perceived risk, complexity, how fast results show up, whether the benefit is visible.
* **Facts about this campaign.** Traffic temperature, mobile share, checkout friction.

Only that last group changes page to page. Record the first two once and inherit them. Re-deciding them every time is how messaging drifts.

Two things that matter more than the list itself:

**Length isn't a property of your product.** If the product is invisible, a supplement say, a good demonstration animation does the work a visual demo would. Build the asset and the page gets shorter.

**Brand equity compounds into shorter pages.** The better known you are, the less the page has to do. Expect to carry more copy early, and test shorter as proof accumulates.

(Georgi is upfront that this framework is partly LLM-expanded and that two of his factors overlap. Use the decomposition, which variables matter and where they live. Don't treat it as a scored instrument.)

## Find the acute symptom of the slow problem

The most portable idea I took from the course.

A deferred benefit is a weak proposition. Anti-aging, "be wealthy in five years," "protect your long-term health." Nobody acts on those today. The move is to find the immediate pain that's symptomatic of the long-term one. The joints that hurt now, the energy gone by 3pm, the few grand a month that gets you out of the job.

This is also what makes a cold headline work. "10 signs your [X] is lower than it should be" converts because you can check it right now. The slow version of the same claim can't.

## How to test landing pages

I like to think of meta ad testing as a pair that you're actually testing. You're not only testing the ad alone, you're testing the combination of the ad and the landing page, because that's the whole experience.

What I've seen work in the past, and you can play around with it, is treat it as a new ad and test it the same way. The idea is for that ad and landing page pair to start gathering spend at an efficiency that works for the business.

One consequence people miss: a creative that flopped on a product page isn't a dead creative. Put it in front of a quiz and it can become your best spender. You tested one pairing, not the ad.

Give the test its own budget. If you drop a new page into an existing pool and it eats all the spend, you haven't gained anything, you traded budget between two ads. Its own allocation makes the comparison clean.

Then use an honest bar, and keep it low on purpose. Can we spend more at the same efficiency, while the existing campaigns hold theirs? When you're shipping promotions, creative, angles and pages all at once, attributing a gain to any single one of them isn't really achievable. Pretending otherwise is worse than admitting it.

On trading efficiency for volume, that's a business question before it's a marketing one. On a consumable with real repeat purchase, more spend at a slightly worse acquisition cost is usually a good trade, you recover it in a cycle or two. On a one-purchase product there's no recovery period, so it's just worse economics. And a spend increase paired with a proportionate cost increase isn't a win at any LTV. You bought volume at the price of the margin that made it worth having.

Ask what your payback window is, and whether the extra volume lands inside it.

## When it's not working: what to check

Let's say you created the ad and the landing page, and you wanna check if it's working or not.

First of all, you need to check for spend and ROAS.

If it works, we are all happy and we can start scaling. But if it doesn't, what do we do?

The first thing is to determine if it's a landing page issue or an ad to landing page coherence issue.

If it's an ad issue, you're probably gonna see higher CPMs and lower CTRs, which will cause cost per visitor to be higher than usual.

If it's a landing page issue, you're probably gonna catch that on conversion rate. At that point, you need to assess if this is worth continuing, playing with another angle, or trying to improve that specific landing page.

You can check screen recordings, heat maps, GA4, funnel analysis, and see where the funnel is breaking.

And now you need to decide if it's worth redoing the landing page based on your analysis, or just kill it and try another template, angle or persona.
